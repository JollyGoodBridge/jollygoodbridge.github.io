Digital Dollars vs Digital Gold

How multinational companies can use stablecoins to move money faster—and why the next contest may be between digital claims on US government debt and digital ownership of physical gold.

By Michael David Lambert Jolly Updated 1 August 2026 About 9 minutes

Stablecoins began as a way for cryptocurrency traders to remain in digital markets without holding volatile assets. They are now moving into payments, remittances and corporate treasury operations. That shift raises a larger question: will the dominant digital money be backed by government debt, physical gold—or both?

The setting: Republica del Peso

Imagine GlobalGoods Inc., a multinational manufacturer selling toothpaste, engine oil and agricultural equipment in a country we will call Republica del Peso. Its local currency is unstable, inflation is high and international bank transfers are slow. Foreign-exchange rules may also restrict when and how local currency can be converted or transferred abroad.

For GlobalGoods, each day between making a sale and obtaining usable hard currency creates uncertainty. The company is not looking for a speculative investment. Its treasury department wants to preserve the value of receipts, know where its cash is and move money efficiently within the law.

The problem: by the time local pesos pass through the banking system and become dollars, fees, exchange-rate movements and delays may have reduced their value. A stablecoin can provide another settlement rail—but it does not remove the company's legal, tax, sanctions or foreign-exchange obligations.

The conventional route may involve several institutions, cut-off times and manual compliance checks. The result can be slower access to working capital and less certainty over the final amount received.

Where local law permits it, an authorised provider can convert the distributor's currency into a dollar-backed payment stablecoin. The tokens can then move across a blockchain network without waiting for each correspondent bank to update its own ledger.

GlobalGoods may redeem the tokens for bank deposits, use them to pay an approved supplier, or retain a limited operational balance. Settlement can be considerably faster, although the complete process still depends on local on-ramps, liquidity, compliance reviews and access to the banking system at each end.

Why Washington now cares

The strategic significance of dollar stablecoins has become much clearer. Under the United States' federal payment-stablecoin framework, permitted reserve assets include cash, deposits, repurchase agreements and short-dated US government securities. Circle says USDC is backed by highly liquid cash and cash-equivalent assets, with most of its reserve held through a government money-market fund. Tether also reports large reserve holdings and publishes quarterly reserve information.

This makes large stablecoin issuers meaningful buyers of short-term US government debt. A growing stock of dollar-backed tokens can therefore increase direct demand for Treasury bills and extend access to dollar-denominated value through internet-based payment rails.

But the effect should not be overstated. Not every new stablecoin dollar represents entirely new financing for the US government. Some users may simply move money from bank deposits, Treasury funds or other dollar assets into a stablecoin. The important development is the creation of a new, globally accessible channel through which demand for dollars and short-term government securities can be expressed.

In July 2025, the US Treasury explicitly described regulated stablecoins as a means of reinforcing the dollar's reserve-currency role and increasing demand for Treasuries. Stablecoin policy has therefore moved beyond cryptocurrency regulation: it has become part of US monetary and financial strategy.

A different proposition: tokenised gold

A gold-backed token does not attempt to remain worth one dollar. Instead, each token represents a defined quantity of physical gold. Paxos, for example, states that one PAX Gold token represents one fine troy ounce of a London Good Delivery gold bar held in professional vaults.

Calling these products “stablecoins” can be misleading. They are stable in relation to a quantity of gold, not in relation to the dollar price of goods, wages or debts. Their dollar value rises and falls with gold. A more precise description is tokenised gold or a gold-backed digital asset.

Digital dollars

  • Designed to maintain a one-dollar redemption value.
  • Suited to invoicing, payroll, trade and settlement.
  • Usually backed by liquid dollar assets, including Treasury bills.
  • Extend the reach of the US dollar and support demand for dollar reserves.
  • Preserve nominal dollars, not necessarily purchasing power.

Digital gold

  • Represents ownership or a claim linked to physical bullion.
  • Suited more naturally to saving, reserves and portfolio diversification.
  • Does not maintain a fixed dollar value.
  • Reduces dependence on the credit of a sovereign borrower, but adds custody risk.
  • Offers gold exposure with blockchain transferability.

The yield question changes the economics

Treasury-backed issuers receive interest from their reserve assets while token holders commonly receive no direct share of that income. This spread gives the issuer a strong and scalable business model.

Physical gold produces no coupon. A gold-token issuer must normally charge custody, transaction or redemption fees. It could try to generate revenue by lending or leasing the metal, but that would introduce borrower and liquidity risks. It could also undermine the central attraction of the product: confidence that the gold is fully present, allocated and available.

The difference is fundamental: a Treasury-backed token can be both fully reserved and income-producing. A conservatively structured gold-backed token can be fully reserved, but the reserve itself produces no income. Sharing a Treasury coupon is economically straightforward; creating a yield on gold usually requires adding risk.

What GlobalGoods would actually use

For GlobalGoods, a dollar stablecoin and a gold-backed token are not interchangeable. Most contracts, accounts and payroll obligations are denominated in currency. A dollar-backed stablecoin is therefore the more natural operational tool.

Gold might have a role in longer-term treasury reserves, particularly where the company wants diversification from dollar purchasing-power risk. It would be less suitable for meeting next week's fixed dollar invoice because its dollar price may move before the payment is due.

Possible uses of a dollar stablecoin

  • Receiving approved cross-border payments more quickly.
  • Paying suppliers that accept the same token.
  • Moving liquidity between approved group entities.
  • Reducing—but not eliminating—correspondent-banking friction.
  • Automating payments subject to treasury and compliance controls.

Possible uses of tokenised gold

  • Holding a limited strategic reserve outside ordinary currency deposits.
  • Transferring fractional gold ownership more easily than physical bars.
  • Providing portfolio exposure to gold without arranging direct vault access.
  • Using gold as collateral where counterparties and local law allow it.

The risks beneath the surface

Issuer and legal-claim risk The token holder must understand exactly who owes what, and whether reserves are legally segregated if the issuer fails.
Reserve and custody risk Cash can be trapped at a failing bank; securities can face liquidity stress; bullion depends on vaulting, title and audit controls.
Redemption risk A market price can depart from the value of the reserve when redemption is restricted, slow, expensive or unavailable to the holder.
Technology risk Wallet errors, compromised keys, smart-contract flaws, network congestion and incompatible blockchains can disrupt access.
Regulatory risk Rules differ by country and can affect issuance, custody, transfers, sanctions screening, tax and the ability to convert into bank money.
Market risk A dollar token preserves a nominal dollar claim; a gold token preserves a gold claim. Neither guarantees stable purchasing power in every economy.

How a prudent treasury would manage them

  • Use regulated or appropriately supervised providers where available.
  • Verify reserve composition, attestations, legal terms and redemption rights.
  • Hold only the operational balance required rather than treating a payment token as an insured bank account.
  • Diversify providers, custody arrangements and settlement routes.
  • Maintain conventional bank access and tested contingency procedures.
  • Apply normal sanctions, anti-money-laundering, tax and foreign-exchange controls.
  • For tokenised gold, confirm whether the metal is allocated, whether it may be lent, and how physical or cash redemption works.

The likely outcome is coexistence, not a winner

Dollar stablecoins and tokenised gold solve different problems. Digital dollars are well adapted to trade, payroll and short-term settlement because obligations are already denominated in currency. Their reserves also create a commercially attractive source of interest income and a new channel of demand for US Treasury bills.

Gold-backed tokens are more naturally a savings or reserve product. They can make ownership of bullion more divisible and transferable, but their value still fluctuates in dollars and their credibility depends on title, custody, audit and redemption.

The emerging contest is therefore not simply “stablecoin versus bank”. It is a choice between different foundations of trust: a redeemable digital claim supported by government debt, and a digital claim to a scarce physical asset.

The most plausible future contains both. Digital dollars may dominate payments; digital gold may compete for savings and reserves.
